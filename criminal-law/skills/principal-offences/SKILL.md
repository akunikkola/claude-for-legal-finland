# Principal offences — label, degree and boundaries

This skill helps identify which offence label an act might fit,
and to distinguish degrees of an offence and neighbouring offences. The map of criminal law:
`../criminal-liability-basics/references/criminal-law-fundamentals.md`.

> **Disclaimer:** the analysis is a draft that needs checking — not legal
> advice. Statutory definitions and sentencing ranges are checked from the source;
> the Criminal Code changes constantly. This domain **does not assist in committing
> or concealing an offence.** See `criminal-law/AGENTS.md`.

## Check the statutory definition from the source — always

This skill gives a **structure**, not the wording of the statutory definition. Retrieve
the definition in force for each label, its degrees and its sentencing range
from the correct chapter of the Criminal Code with the **`legal-core:legal-research` skill**, and the
KKO case law on the boundaries from the source. **The sexual offences chapter
was completely reformed in 2023 (onto a consent basis)** — do not use an
outdated structure.

## Step 1: Which label does the act fit?

Grasp the core of the act and its object, and map the possible labels
**by group** (check each statutory definition from the source):

- **Property offences** — for example theft, petty theft, embezzlement, fraud,
  unauthorised use, criminal damage, usury, robbery (violence + appropriation).
- **Offences against life and health** — for example assault
  (petty/basic/aggravated), negligent bodily injury, homicide offences, abandonment.
- **Economic offences** — for example accounting offences, tax offences (tax fraud),
  debtor's offences, money laundering, bribery offences, securities market
  offences (connection: `banking-and-finance`, `taxation`, `company-law`).
- **Sexual offences** — the reformed chapter (2023), the centrality of
  consent; assess under the statutory definition in force.
- **Narcotics offences** — use, possession, distribution,
  degrees of the offence.
- **Offences against the authorities and the administration of justice** — for example false
  denunciation, false statement, offences in office.
- **Traffic offences** — for example drunken driving, endangering traffic
  safety (partly under their own regulation).

## Step 2: Breaking down the statutory definition

For the chosen label: go through the objective elements and the required
culpability (→ `criminal-liability-basics`). Check whether
each element is satisfied or whether one turns on the evidence or on interpretation.

## Step 3: Degree of the offence — petty, basic or aggravated

- **Grounds of qualification** (aggravated degree) and **privileging**
  (petty) change the sentencing range materially. They generally require both
  a circumstance listed in the law as aggravating/mitigating **and**
  an overall assessment of gravity/pettiness.
- Check the grounds and the criteria for the overall assessment from the source; do not assume
  an aggravated degree from the size of the loss alone.

## Step 4: Drawing the line against neighbouring offences

Typical boundaries (assess through the differences in the statutory definitions,
from the source):

- **Theft vs. embezzlement vs. fraud** — the difference between possession and deception.
- **Fraud vs. breach of contract / a civil dispute** — deception and
  intent; not every unpaid debt is a fraud.
- **Assault vs. self-defence** — repelling an unlawful attack.
- **One act or several** — different labels for one act (concurrence)
  and their effect.

## Step 5: Overall structure

Present: the probable labels, an assessment of whether each statutory definition
is satisfied, the degree of the offence, and the key questions of evidence and boundary.
Mark clearly the points open to interpretation and the need for checking. **Do not
present the outcome as certain** — the presumption of innocence; sentencing ranges from the source.

## What this skill does NOT do

- **Does not assist in committing any offence or in evading a statutory
  definition** — refuse and say why.
- **Does not give the wording of a statutory definition or a sentencing range from memory** —
  from the source.
- **Does not use the outdated structure for sexual offences** (the chapter was reformed
  in 2023).
- **Does not decide guilt or the offence label with binding effect** — that is
  for the prosecutor and the court.
